## Anorexia, Bulimia, and Orthorexia

Change does happen with these diagnoses. The first thing I identify is medical stability. If the patient is not medically stable, outpatient treatment is not an appropriate level of care. I will recommend inpatient treatment and will work with the patient as they access this care. When their body is medically stable, I will continue to provide therapeutic care. Patients that are medically stable are able to make significant changes with outpatient treatment.

I recognize that recovering from an eating disorder requires symptom interruption. Interrupting symptomatic behaviors can be exhausting. I don't want you worn out and not having the ability to fight that nagging voice of an eating disorder. Together we look at the sustainability of interrupting the symptoms necessary to escape an eating disorder.

The constant barrage of negativity in your head, that is so demeaning and constraining is not you, and is not true! That voice is not your child, it is not your spouse, and it is not your parent. That's the voice of an eating disorder and it has you, or your loved one under its influence. The most common denominator of eating disorders is this voice.

I learned how to recognize that voice while working with many patients in an inpatient program. I'm not afraid of the voice and you don't have to be either. My goal for you is that you are able to identify that voice and make the changes needed to separate it from the authentic you.

## Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

I have worked with ARFID impacting patients who;

1. have had an experience with food that caused them to be fearful ie choking allergies, or medical issues.
2. with individuals who lack the hormone ghrelin.
3. with individuals who have heightened sensory issues. These sensory issues often are co-occurring with autism spectrum behaviors.

## Involving Others in Care

The age of the patient and their ability to nourish themselves is significant in treatment planning.

When a young child is experiencing an eating disorder the child is not capable of providing nutrition for themselves. Education, training, and support is focused at the parental level.

The Maudsley approach for teenagers with eating disorders also relies on parental education, training, and support.

Eating disorders often divide those struggling from partners or parents who want to support recovery. It is important to know your enemies and friends. Eating disorders are not friends, even if they are comfortable.

Change is not comfortable as an individual or as a family. It is difficult to initiate and encourage needed change. I often remind families that their voice needs to be louder than the voice of an eating disorder.
